# BROHDA_MANIFESTO.md

**This is not a specification. Not a roadmap. Not a design document.**

This is the constitution. Every future feature, screen, sentence, hire, and business decision gets held up against this document before anything else. If it survives, it ships. If it doesn't, it doesn't — no matter how good the meeting felt, no matter who's asking, no matter how easy it would be to build.

We are not reviewing Brohda anymore. We are protecting it.

---

# Part 1 — Why Brohda Exists

Every group of friends who follows football already has this moment: someone says, with total conviction, "I called it" — and it means nothing. It's a sentence that dies in a group chat, unrecorded, unranked, forgotten by kickoff of the next match. The person who was right gets nothing but the fleeting satisfaction of having said it out loud, once, to people who were already scrolling past.

That's the wound Brohda exists to close.

Sportsbooks solve a different problem entirely — they solve for risk and reward between a stranger and a house, with odds designed to make sure the house doesn't lose. There's no friendship in a sportsbook, and there's no need for one; the whole system is built to work with total anonymity. Prediction markets solve for aggregating a crowd's belief into a price — useful, but the point is the number, not the person who guessed it. Fantasy sports solve for a season-long, roster-management kind of commitment — a real hobby, but not a place for a single, instinctive opinion about this weekend's match to matter today. Reddit solves for conversation, at massive scale, almost entirely among strangers. Instagram solves for self-expression — beautifully — but nothing you post there is ever provably right or wrong.

None of them solve the actual problem: **a person, being provably right, about something they love, in front of people whose opinion of them actually matters — and having that become part of who they are.**

That's not a technology problem. It's a human one. People want to be known for something. Football fans, specifically, already have the raw material for it — an opinion about every match, delivered with total confidence, constantly. Brohda doesn't invent that instinct. It gives it somewhere to live, and something to become.

# Part 2 — The One Sentence

Start broad:

*"Brohda is a social platform where football fans compete against their friends by predicting match outcomes for a small, fixed stake, and build a public reputation for their football knowledge over time."*

Cut what isn't load-bearing:

*"Brohda is where friends compete to prove who knows football best."*

Cut again:

*"Prove you know football best."*

Cut until nothing moves:

**"Prove it."**

That's the sentence. Two words. It's the internal definition of the product, and it's the test every other word in this document, and every word Brohda ever ships, has to survive.

# Part 3 — The Core Emotion

Nobody remembers a feature list. They remember how something made them feel. These are the feelings Brohda is responsible for, in the order they should arrive.

**Curiosity.** Why it matters: it's the reason someone opens the app in the first place, with nothing on the line. Where it appears: the feed, the sentiment bar, a friend's profile. How it's strengthened: by always having something genuinely worth wondering about visible in the first five seconds. How it's weakened: by any screen that answers a question before the user got to ask it — a leaderboard defaulted to a view nobody chose, a card that tells you the outcome before you've formed an opinion.

**Anticipation.** Why it matters: it's the feeling that makes someone come back before they know the answer. Where it appears: the moment right after a prediction is made, all the way to kickoff. How it's strengthened: by treating the waiting period as a real, designed part of the experience, not dead time between two screens. How it's weakened: by silence — nothing in the product currently marks this feeling as belonging to anyone, so it belongs to no one.

**Pride.** Why it matters: it's the payoff, and it's the entire point. Where it appears: the moment a prediction resolves correctly, in public, where people can see it. How it's strengthened: by giving that moment real visual and emotional weight — never a receipt, always a headline. How it's weakened: by treating a win the same, in tone and prominence, as a routine system notification.

**Belonging.** Why it matters: it's what makes the stakes feel warm instead of transactional. Where it appears: comments, follows, avatar stacks, the simple fact of a feed full of people you actually know. How it's strengthened: by always putting a person's face and name ahead of any number on the same screen. How it's weakened: by any surface that treats a user as an account rather than a person — forms, settings, jargon.

**Recognition.** Why it matters: it's what turns a private feeling of being right into a public fact about who you are. Where it appears: identity, reputation, the leaderboard. How it's strengthened: by making sure a good track record is *visible* wherever a person's name already appears, not buried behind a tap. How it's weakened: by fragmenting it across too many views, or diluting it with participation-trophy inflation.

**Competition.** Why it matters: it's the engine — without something at stake, there's nothing to be right or wrong about. Where it appears: entry, the leaderboard, rivalries between specific people. How it's strengthened: by keeping stakes equal and fixed for everyone, every time. How it's weakened: by anything that lets money buy an advantage, or that turns the competition into a system rather than a person-versus-person contest.

**Conversation.** Why it matters: it's the difference between a bet and a story worth telling. Where it appears: comments, mentions, replies. How it's strengthened: by making sure the loudest thing on every screen is always a person's words, never a status code. How it's weakened: by any moment — a void, a refund, a settlement — communicated in a voice a person would never actually use.

**Trust.** Why it matters: without it, none of the above is safe to feel. Where it appears: the wallet, settlement, every moment money is involved. How it's strengthened: by never letting a user wonder, even briefly, whether something worked. How it's weakened: by silence, by delay without explanation, by jargon standing in for a plain sentence.

# Part 4 — The Product Pyramid

```
                People
                  ↓
             Conversation
                  ↓
              Reputation
                  ↓
              Predictions
                  ↓
               Football
                  ↓
                Money
                  ↓
             Administration
```

This order is not arbitrary, and it is not negotiable.

**People come first** because nothing in this product means anything without them — a prediction with no one to see it is just a private guess. **Conversation comes second** because it's the mechanism that turns a private guess into a shared, memorable moment. **Reputation comes third** because it's what conversation compounds into over time — the lasting result of being right, publicly, again and again. **Predictions come fourth** because they're the raw material everything above them is built from, but they are a means, not the end. **Football comes fifth** because it's the subject matter — real, specific, and necessary, but the product is not "an app about football," it's a product about *people*, using football as the shared language. **Money comes sixth** because it's what makes the stakes real, but it is never what the product is *for*. **Administration comes last** because it exists solely to keep everything above it running, and it should never be visible to anyone above the line.

Every future screen is judged against this order. If a screen makes Money more visually dominant than People, it is already wrong, regardless of how well it was built. If a feature strengthens something low on this pyramid at the cost of something higher, it does not ship.

# Part 5 — Identity

People should become known for something. Not by choosing a title. Not by unlocking a badge. Not by paying for a flair. **By being right, repeatedly, in a specific, describable way — and having the product notice.**

**How identity should emerge.** It comes entirely from behavior, computed over a rolling window of real, settled predictions — never self-declared, never permanent. If someone consistently backs the underdog correctly, that's an identity. If someone reads draws better than anyone else in their circle, that's an identity. The system watches for a pattern that's statistically distinctive — not lucky, not average, genuinely and repeatably different — and it names it.

**How people become memorable.** By having that name attached to them wherever their name already appears — on a profile, in a comment on a match the identity is relevant to. Memorability isn't manufactured with a notification; it's a byproduct of visibility done honestly, in context.

**How reputations spread.** Through conversation, always — someone mentioning what another person is known for, in a comment, unprompted, because it's true and it's interesting. A reputation system that has to advertise itself has already failed; a real one gets talked about because it's accurate.

**How stories naturally form.** Every identity is, structurally, a story waiting to happen: a Draw Specialist calling a draw everyone else missed; an Underdog Hunter backing the team nobody believed in and being right again. The story isn't manufactured — it's the natural, human reaction to watching a pattern hold, or watching it finally break.

**How rivalries emerge.** Two people chasing the same identity, in the same circle, is a rivalry the product doesn't need to design — it only needs to make visible. When someone's form overtakes someone else's in the same category, that handoff, shown plainly, is the rivalry.

**How users should describe each other.** The way people describe each other in real life — by nickname, by reputation, in the third person, with a little bit of pride or teasing behind it. "He's the Comeback King" is a sentence a person says about a friend. "User 4821 has a 63% accuracy rate in category 7" is not, and never should be what this system produces or implies.

**What should never exist, because it would feel artificial:** any identity tied to how much money someone has spent, how many pools they've entered regardless of accuracy, how long they've had an account, or anything a person could simply buy, grind, or wait out. The moment an identity can be earned through volume or money instead of genuine accuracy, it stops being a reputation and starts being a leaderboard for spend — and that is precisely the thing this system exists to avoid becoming.

Identity is Brohda's most important unbuilt idea. Protect its authenticity above every other consideration, including how fast it could ship a shortcut version.

# Part 6 — Simplicity, Rewritten for Brohda

**One prediction is better than ten.** Depth of feeling beats breadth of choice. A product that offers one great, well-considered decision will out-engage a product offering ten mediocre ones, every time.

**Every setting is guilty until proven innocent.** The default answer to "should this be configurable" is no. A setting only earns its existence with real, repeated, specific demand — never with a hypothetical.

**Every tap must earn its place.** If a step can be removed without losing meaning, it's not a step, it's friction wearing a step's clothes.

**Every number must answer a question someone is actually asking.** A number on screen that nobody was wondering about is decoration pretending to be information.

**Every screen has one purpose.** If a screen's purpose takes more than one sentence to state, it's actually two screens that haven't been separated yet, or one screen that's trying to do too much.

**If it needs an explanation, it has already failed.** A rule pill, a tooltip, a help sheet — every one of these is evidence that something upstream wasn't designed clearly enough the first time.

**Two ways to do the same thing is one too many.** Wherever the product offers a duplicate path to the same idea — two profile views, two history views, two ways to see who you're facing — one of them is dead weight, and dead weight is not neutral; it's a permanent tax on every future person who has to understand the product, including future engineers building on top of it.

# Part 7 — The Feed

The feed is Brohda. Not a feature of Brohda — the thing itself. Every other screen exists to feed it, explain it, or protect it.

**What belongs on it:** people, their picks, the sentiment of the group, the conversation happening around a prediction, the honest stakes involved. Anything that helps someone decide, or feel something, or want to say something back.

**What never belongs on it:** settings, visibility metadata, raw system status, anything explaining how the software works instead of what a person did. If an element requires the reader to understand something about Brohda's internals to make sense of it, it does not belong on the feed, under any circumstance.

**What's always visually dominant, in this exact order:** the person first — a name, a face, always the first thing the eye lands on. Then the shared moment — the match, the pick, the sentiment of the group. Money is present, because it's real and it's honest, but it is never the loudest thing on a card, and it never will be. This ordering is the Product Pyramid (Part 4) made literal, on the one screen that matters most.

# Part 8 — Money

Money is necessary. Money is not the product.

**How money should feel:** transparent, simple, and secondary — always visible when someone genuinely needs to see it, and never louder than the person or the moment it's attached to.

**When money should disappear:** at the moment of deciding what to predict. That decision should be driven entirely by conviction about football, not by arithmetic. The instant money starts competing with instinct for attention, something has gone wrong.

**When money should become prominent:** exactly twice — when someone is deciding to commit (so the stakes are honestly understood) and after a result settles (so exactly where every dollar went is never a mystery). Full, unambiguous transparency at both moments, no exceptions, no hidden fees, no soft language.

**How we avoid looking like a sportsbook:** no odds, no spreads, no "the smart money says," no house-edge framing, ever, anywhere a player can see it. The moment Brohda starts talking about probability instead of belief, it has started becoming something it swore it would never be.

**How we avoid looking like a bank:** no forms that feel like an interrogation, no silence where a sentence would do, no bureaucratic waiting with no stated expectation. A bank makes you prove yourself to it. Brohda should never make a user feel like they have to prove themselves to their own wallet.

# Part 9 — Trust

Trust is not won in a single grand gesture. It's earned or lost in dozens of tiny moments a user barely notices consciously — and loses all at once the first time one of them goes wrong silently.

**How Brohda communicates, always:** plainly, immediately, and like a person talking to another person. Never a status code standing in for a sentence. Never an assumption that silence will be read as "everything's fine" — silence is always read as risk, and it should never be asked to carry that weight.

**Delays:** stated, with an honest expectation, the moment they begin — not discovered by a user wondering why nothing happened.

**Refunds:** always the same, simple shape, regardless of the dozen different reasons behind them. A person doesn't need to understand *why* a match didn't count to trust that they got their money back — they need to be told, once, clearly, that they did.

**Wins:** treated like the best thing that happens in this product, because they are. Never a receipt. Always a moment.

**Losses:** honest, brief, and never cold. A small sting is fine — that's what makes winning feel like something. Confusion is never fine.

**Errors:** named plainly, with what to do next stated in the same sentence. Never "something went wrong" alone.

**What should never be hidden:** the fee, where money is, why a decision was made, what happens next. **What should never be over-explained:** internal mechanics, technical grading logic, anything that exists purely to keep the system correct on the backend. A user should always know *that* something is being handled correctly, and should almost never need to know *how*.

# Part 10 — UX Philosophy, As Law

**Confusion is a bug.** Not a rough edge, not a minor issue — a bug, with the same priority as anything that crashes.

**Silence creates anxiety.** Every moment the product could speak and doesn't is a moment it's asking the user to trust it blindly. It should never ask that.

**Every decision costs attention.** Attention is the scarcest resource this product has from any given user in any given session. It is never to be spent carelessly.

**Every interaction should increase confidence, never decrease it.** If a tap leaves someone less sure of what just happened than before they tapped, that interaction is broken, regardless of whether it technically worked.

**Every simplification should increase emotion.** Removing a step is not the goal by itself — removing a step so that the feeling underneath it arrives faster and clearer is the goal. A simplification that flattens the feeling along with the friction is not a simplification. It's a loss.

**A feature that requires a tutorial has already failed.** If understanding a feature requires being taught, the feature was designed for the system's convenience, not the user's.

**If a user has to ask "did that work," the product has already lost that moment**, no matter what the eventual answer turns out to be.

# Part 11 — Product Boundaries

**We are not a sportsbook.** No odds, no spreads, no variable stakes, no house edge, no anonymity between strangers. We will not add these things even if they would make more money, because more money is not the goal — being unmistakably, permanently different from a sportsbook is.

**We are not fantasy football.** No season-long roster management, no ongoing strategic commitment required to participate in a single moment. Brohda is about one match, one opinion, one outcome — not a hobby that demands a season of upkeep.

**We are not a prediction market.** We do not price belief. The sentiment bar shows what the room thinks because that's a social fact worth knowing, not because it's an instrument to be traded.

**We are not social media for everything.** We are not trying to be the place people post about their lives, their opinions on anything, their day. We are the place they prove they know football, with people who already know them.

**We are football-first.** Not sports-first, not "prediction-of-anything"-first. The domain is specific on purpose — it's what makes the identity system meaningful and the conversation genuine, rather than generic.

**We are mobile-first.** Brohda is something checked in a pocket, between other things, not something opened on a desktop and studied. Every design decision assumes a thumb, not a mouse.

**We are conversation-first.** Every feature is judged by whether it puts two people in a conversation, or one person alone in a settings menu. The former is always preferred, without exception.

These boundaries are not limitations we're waiting to outgrow. They are the shape of the product. Protect them exactly as hard as anything this document calls "core" — a boundary that gets quietly eroded one exception at a time is indistinguishable, in the end, from a boundary that was never real.

# Part 12 — The Founder Filter

Every new feature request runs this gauntlet, in this order, before anything else is discussed:

Does this strengthen identity? Does it create conversation? Does it create a story someone would tell a friend? Does it remove friction? Does it strengthen reputation? Does it increase anticipation? Would users tell friends about it, unprompted? Does it make Brohda easier to explain, not harder? Would removing it, six months from now, make the product stronger, not weaker? Would Steve Jobs approve of it, or would he delete it on sight? Would Kevin Systrom ship it, or would he say it feels like software, not a social product? Would Brian Chesky understand, immediately, why it exists — or would he ask what problem of trust or belonging it's actually solving?

**If any honest answer is "no," the feature should probably not be built.** Not "build a smaller version of it." Not "build it but hide it." Do not build it, and revisit the underlying need with a completely different idea if the need is still real once the bad version is off the table.

# Part 13 — Design Philosophy

**Instagram-inspired, because that visual language is already the fastest way to say "this is social, this is about people" without a single word of copy.** We inherit it deliberately, not by accident.

**Calm, because a product about proving you're right shouldn't itself be anxious.** Loud interfaces manufacture urgency; Brohda's urgency should come from the match clock, never from the UI competing for attention against itself.

**Premium, because trust and quality are inseparable.** A product that looks cheap makes people wonder if their money is safe, even when it is. Craft is not decoration here — it's part of the trust system.

**Human, because every screen is ultimately about a relationship between people, mediated by software, never the other way around.**

**Whitespace, because restraint is confidence.** A screen that's afraid of empty space is a screen that doesn't trust its own content to be interesting on its own.

**Content first, people first — always, on every screen, in that order of appearance, never violated by a settings panel, a status label, or an administrative concern that happened to be easy to build.**

# Part 14 — The Emotional Timeline

**Opening Brohda:** curiosity, immediately, before a single tap.
**Scrolling:** curiosity sustained, occasionally sharpening into a specific opinion about a specific card.
**Reading predictions:** quick comprehension, never a second read required to understand what's being asked.
**Choosing:** a small, real decision — enough deliberation to feel like a genuine opinion, never so much that it feels like homework.
**Entering:** commitment, deliberate and weighted, never rushed, never accidental.
**Waiting:** anticipation that has somewhere to live — a conversation, a countdown, a reason to check back, not a void.
**Watching:** shared tension, ideally alongside the people who are watching the same thing.
**Winning:** pride, loud enough to notice, public enough to matter.
**Losing:** a small sting, honestly delivered, never confusing, never punishing.
**Being refunded:** calm reassurance — this didn't count, here's your money, nothing more needed.
**Checking the leaderboard:** pride or motivation, delivered instantly, with no decision required to see it.
**Closing the app:** the sense that something is still unfinished — a live streak, an open prediction, a reputation still being written — never a sense of finality.

Every one of these transitions should feel intentional. If a transition currently feels like nothing, that's not neutral — it's a designed feeling failing to be designed, and it should be treated with the same seriousness as a bug.

# Part 15 — The Ten Commandments

Immutable. A pull request, a mockup, or a product idea that violates one of these gets rejected on sight, regardless of who proposed it or how good the rest of the work is.

1. **Every entry fee is fixed and equal. Money never buys a bigger victory.**
2. **The feed always comes first. Nothing competes with it for the first five seconds of a session.**
3. **People are shown before statistics, on every screen, without exception.**
4. **Identity is earned through real behavior, never chosen, never bought, never permanent.**
5. **Silence is never an acceptable answer to "did that work."**
6. **Every setting removed is worth more than every setting added.**
7. **We will never speak like a sportsbook, and we will never operate like a bank.**
8. **A feature that creates no emotion and no conversation does not belong in this product.**
9. **Simplicity and engagement are never in conflict here — if a proposal treats them as a trade-off, the proposal is wrong, not the principle.**
10. **The one-sentence identity — "Prove it." — is the final word in every disagreement. When in doubt, it wins.**

# Part 16 — The North Star

Ten years from now, Brohda is one of the most loved football apps in the world. Not the biggest. Not the one with the most features, or the most markets, or the most revenue per user. The most *loved* — the one people bring up unprompted, the one that gets recommended in exactly the way it was born to be recommended: a friend, telling another friend, because something happened inside it that was worth repeating out loud.

It's loved because it never tried to be everything. It stayed football, it stayed friends, it stayed a small, fixed, honest stake and a public, permanent, earned record of who was right. It said no to a hundred good ideas because none of them were *this* idea, and it turned out that saying no, consistently, for a decade, is what made the yes so sharp.

It's loved because of a feeling nobody can quite name the source of when they try to explain it to someone who hasn't used it — something about the exact instant a prediction resolves in your favor, in front of people whose opinion of your football knowledge actually matters to you, and the quiet, permanent satisfaction of watching that become part of who you are to them. Not a badge. Not a trophy. A reputation, earned the only way a real one ever is — by being right, again, in public, when it mattered.

It's loved because the money always felt safe, the wallet never once made anyone wait in silence, and the fee was never a surprise. Because the feed always looked like a room full of people who actually knew each other, never like a market. Because every screen said less than it could have, and meant more because of it.

This is the product every person who ever works on Brohda is protecting. Not a roadmap item. Not a metric. A feeling — specific, earned, and impossible to get anywhere else — that we owe it to every user who ever says "I called it" to make sure the product remembers it too.

**Prove it.**
